Three ways I approach AI implementation

Short, inspectable examples of turning business requirements into bounded workflows, handling failure, and documenting evidence. These are portfolio demonstrations and reported engineering investigations—not client deployments.

01 · Implementation design

From a request to an auditable workflow

Problem
AI workflows can appear complete even when approvals, execution, and evidence are disconnected.
Approach
Model separate request, permission, approval, execution, and verification stages; provide an interactive walkthrough of the human decision gate.
Proof
Browser-only Control Room simulation and six-stage Orchestration Atlas; their public behavior can be inspected and reproduced.
Outcome
A reviewable explanation of where an operator approves or rejects work—not proof of live agent execution.

02 · Customer onboarding

Consent, approval, retries, and escalation

Problem
An onboarding process must not treat missing consent or unhealthy integrations as success.
Approach
Build a deterministic Python scenario matrix with consent and approval checks, bounded retry recommendations, and human escalation.
Proof
24 fictional scenario combinations and five additional assertions are documented with commands for local reproduction; CI history determines actual run status.
Outcome
A testable teaching example for implementation and customer success—not a deployed CRM integration or measured business improvement.

03 · Human oversight

Preventing approval reuse and scope escalation

Problem
An approval for one command must not silently authorize a materially edited command or a mixed authorized/unauthorized request.
Approach
Investigate immutable approval binding, normalized scope, cancellation isolation, unknown-intent rejection, and positive/negative test controls.
Proof
A private XMAS-29 assessment reported 66 authorization cases, including significant failures in canonical and A7 candidates. The underlying private tests are not publicly reproducible.
Outcome
Clear remediation and release criteria, with authorization qualification still open—not a security certification.
